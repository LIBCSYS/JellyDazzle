# 135 Kelvin Wake

**Look.** Two ships you never see, each trailing a hard-edged V of nested crescent waves
across black water, the two wakes sweeping in opposite directions and crossing each other.
Inside each V the crests are stacked arcs; along the outer arms they break into a fine
feathered fringe. A single bright glint marks each hull at the apex. Sparse and graphic —
93% near-black, and the most saturated frame in this batch — it reads as an overlay rather
than a water surface.

**Math.** The wake half-angle is 19.47 degrees regardless of speed — Kelvin's result — and it
falls out of the deep-water dispersion: a wave train holding station with a hull moving at
`V`, at angle `theta` to the track, must have wavenumber `k = k0 sec^2(theta)`. This pattern
sums 120 plane waves with exactly those wavenumbers and nothing else, so the two families
the eye reads — transverse crests strung across the track (small `theta`) and divergent
crests fanning off it (large `theta`) — are not drawn separately: they are the two
stationary-phase branches of the same integral, and the cusp line where they meet is the
19.47-degree arm. `theta` is sampled uniformly over `+-1.0` rad with only the truncation of
the family tapered; weighting the ends any harder makes the divergent branch vanish. The
wake is cut at `0.70 AH` astern, which is where the phase step between adjacent `theta`
approaches `pi` and the divergent branch would start to alias. Amplitude peaks toward the
cusp arm, not on the track, and falls as `1/sqrt(1 + 0.055 d)`. Everything outside the wedge
is skipped, which is both physically right and what makes 240 waves per pixel affordable:
phases accumulate incrementally along a row against a cosine table and are reseeded once per
row, at the point where the wedge goes live. Crests are lit by a cubed half-cosine so the
troughs stay black. 320x240 canvas, bilinear upscale. Measured 263 fps at 1280x960, delta
0.09-0.11.
