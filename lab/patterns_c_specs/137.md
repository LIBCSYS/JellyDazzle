# 137 Rutt Etra Relief

**Look.** A landscape made of nothing but its own scanlines: ninety-odd horizontal ribbons,
each bent upward where the terrain rises, bunching toward a horizon and spreading toward
the viewer. Because near ridges genuinely hide what is behind them, it reads as a solid
lit relief rather than a stack of curves, and the shadowed troughs are true black. One
broad swell wanders through the field like a subject moving under a sheet, and the terrain
scrolls slowly toward the camera. Ridge crests catch a highlight; altitude sets the hue, so
peaks and valleys sit in different parts of the palette.

**Math.** The Rutt/Etra Scan Processor (1972) deflected a CRT's raster with a video signal,
turning a picture into a ribbed 3D relief. This is that instrument driven by a synthetic
terrain. 96 rows are placed in perspective at world depth `z = 1.05 + d^2 * 6.4` (squared, so
rows bunch toward the horizon), with screen span, row spacing and displacement amplitude all
proportional to `1/z`. The height field is four travelling sinusoids in `(u, v)` plus one
broad wandering Gaussian swell; `v` is the row's world coordinate minus a scroll term, so
the terrain advances. Rows are rasterised front to back through a per-column horizon buffer
— the voxel-terrain hidden-line rule — and a sample is drawn only where it rises above the
horizon already established, which is what makes near ridges occlude far ones. Steps taller
than a pixel are filled with a short vertical run so the ribbon never breaks. Brightness
combines a slope term (`1/(1 + 0.55|dy|)`, so flat crests read specular) with the depth
factor; hue indexes the palette on height. 384x288 float canvas, 3x3 bloom, bilinear
upscale. Measured 330 fps at 1280x960, delta 1.14-1.57.
