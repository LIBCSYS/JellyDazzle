# 195 — Aperture Star

Fraunhofer diffraction through a slowly turning polygon. Far-field diffraction from an
n-sided aperture throws one ray perpendicular to each edge — the reason every photograph
of a bright light wears a fringed star, and the reason the ray count is 2n for even n.
The frame builds that model directly rather than transforming an image: for each of n
edge normals, project the pixel onto the ray to get (p,q), then apply a Gaussian across
the ray and a fringed envelope `(1 + cos 2 sigma p)/(1 + 0.42 sigma p)` along it. The
strict sinc-squared of the physics falls off as 1/p^2 and is invisible past its first
lobe, so this uses the amplitude envelope instead, which keeps the beaded rays legible
all the way to the frame edge while preserving the fringe spacing. Under it sits an
Airy-like ring core, `exp(-0.62 r)` times a squared cosine in r that rotates outward.
Three sample wavelengths at 0.84, 1.00 and 1.20 are traced separately: the long one
reaches further and rings wider, so each ray fans into a small spectrum and the halo
bands. The spike geometry does not depend on wavelength, so p and q are computed once per
pixel per ray with an early-out that rejects the pixel for all three wavelengths at once —
that single restructuring took the pattern from 122 to 192 fps. The aperture turns at
0.0004 to 0.0009 radians a frame; a mask kills the centre so all n rays cannot pile up
into a white blob. 192 fps, mean frame delta 0.08 — the second-quietest here — 87%
near-black and the most saturated thing in the set. Screen-blend overlay.
