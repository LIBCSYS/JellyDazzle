JellyDazzle

A DAZZLE.EXE homage for Apple Silicon, built with AI-guided coding — 24 engine modes in hand-written ARM64 assembly, 610 patterns in C. It listens to whatever is playing — on your Mac, iPhone or iPad — and paints to it: never the same pattern, never the same colours.

What it looks like

Play music first
JellyDazzle is audio-reactive — put something on before you watch it. Anything playing on your Mac will do: a record, a film, a livestream. On macOS 14.2+ it listens to the system output, so it follows whatever app is making the sound. In silence it still runs, but on internal clocks — and you are seeing maybe half of what it does.

Signed by Apple
This download is code-signed as Developer ID Application: LIBCSYSTEMS LLC and notarised by Apple, with the ticket stapled so it verifies offline. It installs and opens with no warnings — no “cannot verify”, no right-click trick. On the Mac App Store since October 8, 2026.

How a frame is built

Up to four layers run at once, bottom to top: a ground fills the screen, a field covers most of it with gaps, a figure drops distinct shapes over that, and a spark adds glints. They blend by brightness, so dark parts of an upper layer let the one below show through.

Each layer takes a turn of its own length, then a new routine is drawn from a shuffled bag and fades in. Timing is random by design — never a fixed loop. At startup the engine measures every routine and assigns it a layer by how much of the screen it actually covers.

Where things stand

A few people hit real problems with the earlier downloads. They are fixed, and it is worth being straight about what they were.

  • It would not open on older Macs. The build inherited the macOS version of the machine that compiled it, so a copy built on the newest system simply refused to launch on anything earlier — which meant the download only worked for people already running the latest OS. The floor is now macOS 11, set explicitly and pinned in the bundled SDL2 as well, so what you download runs on hardware older than the machine that built it. Resolved in 2.7.
  • It looked frozen when you first opened it. On first run JellyDazzle measures every routine in the library and probes the available audio devices before it draws anything, which on some Macs takes 30–60 seconds with nothing on screen. It was working, not hung. Those measurements are now cached and there is a count-up clock and a cancel, so it says what it is doing and every later launch is immediate. Resolved in 2.7.
  • Patterns repeated themselves. Two routines from the same shape family could end up on screen together, which made a library of hundreds feel smaller than it was. Families are now tracked and never share the screen. Resolved in 2.7.
  • macOS said it “cannot verify” the app. Fixed in 3.0.2. Every earlier download was signed only ad-hoc, so Gatekeeper could not check who made it and warned you. The current release is signed as Developer ID Application: LIBCSYSTEMS LLC and notarised by Apple, with the ticket stapled so it works offline. Download it, open it, done — no warning, no right-click trick.

Now on iPhone and iPad

The same engine — the same assembly core and all 634 routines — runs on iPhone and iPad. Version 3.5.8 is on the App Store now. Put a song on in Spotify or Apple Music, open JellyDazzle and set the phone down: your music keeps playing at full volume while JellyDazzle listens through the microphone and paints to it.

  • Lite on battery, Full when charging. On battery, in Low Power Mode, or when the phone is warm it draws a softer picture at 30 frames a second; plug it in and it becomes full JellyDazzle — 60 frames, every layer. It switches on its own, mid-show.
  • C, S and ? buttons in the corner for colours, shapes and help. They brighten when you touch the screen and fade while you watch. A tap gives new colours, two fingers new shapes, press and hold shows what is playing.

Your own pictures, the feature announced here before, is on hold — the phones came first. Get it on the App Store, or follow along at github.com/LIBCSYS/JellyDazzle.

What's new

  • 3.5.8 — iPhone and iPad, and buttons. Released Oct 9, 2026 — Mac, iPhone and iPad. The whole engine now runs on iOS, with Lite and Full power modes so it is kind to a battery, and your music keeps playing while it listens. New C, S and ? buttons sit in the corner (and X on the Mac) so nobody has to know the keys. The background now rotates through far more variety — the same family of pattern never plays twice in a row — and the JD mark appears once, when JellyDazzle opens, instead of with every new set of shapes. Getting it onto a phone meant adopting iOS 27’s new app lifecycle, which the first test build did not survive: it closed before it drew a frame. The crash report came back through TestFlight within minutes, and build 7 fixed it.
  • 3.0.4 — the accent layers were never being drawn. On a Retina display you were seeing a background and, at most, one layer on top of it. The small bright accent and spark layers — the ones that give the picture its snap — never appeared at all. Not dim, not washed out: never drawn. A load valve that thins the stack when frames get expensive was latching the first time a frame ran long and then never releasing, because releasing needed 120 fast frames in a row and at full Retina resolution that never happens. One early hiccup removed half the layer stack for the rest of the session. Measured on the headless harness at 3456×2160, the accent layer was present in 0.0% of frames before this build. Layers now enter at half rate under load instead of being refused outright. The contrast and fog tuning behind this is still being measured rather than guessed — that part is not finished.
  • 3.0.3 — patterns keep going past their own edge. A pattern that zoomed out or drifted across the screen used to tow a hard black rectangle behind it — you could see the box it was drawn in. Everything outside the pattern was being filled with solid black. It now mirrors instead, so the pattern reflects at its own edge and carries on, and the box is gone. The first-run notice is also honest now: it used to say “press space to continue anyway”, which suggested skipping it stopped the database build. It never did.
  • 3.0.2 — signed, and two keys that drive the show. The download is now signed and notarised by Apple, so it opens with no warning at all. C changes the colour and S changes the shapes — neither cuts, both fade, because nothing here strobes. C used to pick the single most-distant scheme, which sounds right but is deterministic and symmetric: it toggled between two palettes and the other 178 never appeared. It now chooses at random among the most distant, never repeating the one it just left. There is also a real Mac menu bar — Help on ⌘?, with the site and support contact, because macOS has no F1.
  • 2.8.0 — fractals. Seven new routines, each a different visual language: 604 Mandel Deep, 605 Julia Morph — a Julia set that genuinely changes shape while you watch, its constant walking through ten curated values while the plane is re-swept fourteen scanlines a frame, forever — 606 Burning Ship, 607 Newton Basins, 608 Lyapunov, 609 Buddhabrot, which exposes over about a minute like a photographic plate rather than arriving finished, and 610 Flame IFS, whose attractor is drawn from the seed so every instance is one that has probably never been rendered before. None of them paint anything black: interiors are coloured by orbit trap and exteriors by trap-folded escape, so the whole frame is live colour. They share a shape family, so two never run at once.
  • 2.7 — the whole library. All 400 held candidate patterns promoted into the shipping build — no work discarded — taking it from 201 plug-ins to 610. Shape repetition fixed: two routines of the same family can no longer share the screen. The startup database is genuinely built once, with a count-up clock and a cancel. The macOS floor dropped from 26 to 11, so the download runs on hardware older than the machine that built it.
  • 2.6 — the mark, and movement. Adaptive mobility: the engine measures how much each routine moves on its own and gives the stillest ones the most transform, so nothing sits static.
  • 2.5.4 — surprise. Every layer used to take a window into one shared palette ramp, blended from just two curated schemes — so every colour on screen came from the same two palettes, and the engine was architecturally incapable of clashing or of opposites. About 30% of overlay layers now break out, taking a scheme of their own and optionally rotating hue 120° or 240° (luma-preserving, so nothing dims). The result is pairings the curated schemes would never produce: red on blue, magenta on green. The ground stays coherent on purpose. The palettes are a guide now, not a law.
  • 2.5.3 — advanced colour management. Rotation moves a pattern through its own window of the ramp, so a narrow span means little hue travels. The startup probe now measures colour diversity and gives flat, graphic routines up to 2.4× the span. Only 9% of the library is flat — and those are exactly the ones that looked static. They don't change colour less; a flat region just becomes a different flat colour, and nothing sweeps. Morph needs gradients.
  • 2.5.2 — even spread, breathing silence. The memory ring covers every spawn now, budgeted per role: routines repeating 4+ times across eight runs fell from 15 to 7. And silence gets its own rhythm from three clocks that never line up, instead of going flat when the music stops.
  • 2.5.1 — the Dazzle method. 602 String Envelope is the first pattern that works the way the original did: it draws a composition once into a plane of palette indices, then animates for its whole turn by moving the palette underneath. The geometry never moves. Only the colour travels. That is how DAZZLE.EXE got such rich motion out of an 8088, and video of it running shows eight of twelve sampled frames holding identical geometry with only the colour sweeping (the story). The content is the original's workhorse primitive, line-sweep string art — every line straight, the envelope they trace curved. Per-frame cost: one table lookup per pixel.
    Press A and the app now names every layer on screen, so you can tell exactly what you are looking at.
  • 2.4.4 — layers always read apart. A layer's palette window used to be picked at random with no regard for what was already on screen, so an overlay could land on the same hue family as the ground and vanish. Measured across 232 concurrent pairs, the closest sat at 0.0% — identical. A spawning layer now takes the furthest of eight candidate windows; worst separation is 17.6%, with nothing under 15%.
  • 2.4.3 — it never opens on the same thing. For the first couple of seconds only one layer is on screen, so first impressions came from a single bag of ~77 backgrounds drawn with no memory between launches — a repeat roughly every ten runs. It now remembers its last 25 openings and refuses them. Thirty launches, thirty unique openings.
  • 2.4.2 — the colour never stops. Palette rotation was driven entirely by audio, so in silence the hues morphed but stopped travelling. There's now an idle floor of about one turn of the ramp every 50 seconds, with audio stacking on top. Taken straight from video of the original (see the tribute), where eight of twelve sampled frames are the same geometry with only the colour moving.
  • 2.4.1 — an icon, and an About card. Press A for version, source, library and the key map.
  • 2.4.0 — system audio, no strobe, solid fullscreen. Captures what your Mac is actually playing through a Core Audio process tap. Layer palette windows are cyclic, so audio-driven rotation can't drag a pattern across a discontinuity. Fullscreen no longer restarts the engine or crashes. Startup measurements are cached, so every launch begins with the whole library sorted into layers.

Build it yourself

git clone https://github.com/LIBCSYS/JellyDazzle.git
cd JellyDazzle
make          # build
make run      # build and launch
make app      # signed self-contained .app

Needs only the Xcode command line tools (xcode-select --install). SDL2 ships in the repository, built against the minimum supported macOS — deliberately, because a Homebrew SDL is compiled for whichever macOS the build machine happens to run and silently pins the finished app to it. Apple Silicon only; the 24 engine modes are hand-written ARM64 assembly (the 610 patterns and the compositor are C). macOS asks for microphone permission on first launch so the visuals can move with your music — they run on internal clocks without it. First launch can take 30–60 seconds while it probes audio devices; it is working, not hung.

In tribute

JellyDazzle exists because of DAZZLE.EXE, written by James R. Shiflett of Houston, Texas. He wrote it at night — “a sort of therapy,” he called it — while his day job was administrative work around an NEC SX-2 supercomputer at the Houston Advanced Research Center.

MicroTronics released it as shareware in 1990 for $15. Road Scholar Software published it at retail as Razzle Dazzle in 1992, and by 1993 it was earning more in shareware registrations each week than most people made in a month. It went head to head with After Dark, and in 1993 its visuals became a 45-minute art film scored by Jonn Serrie.

Its source was never released. This is not a port and not a reverse engineering — nothing was disassembled. It is an homage written from scratch, chasing the same feeling: cheap geometry, mirrored symmetry, and colour that moves because the palette moves rather than because the pixels do.

Shiflett’s exit splash read:
“Our Creator, evidenced by our creativity.”
